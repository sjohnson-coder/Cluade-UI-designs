Part 02 · The Telegram storm

One missing word

Five identical red alerts in one minute. The dedupe system that should have collapsed them is working perfectly — on a class your message never reaches.

_telegram_minimal_category() routes a message into the throttled rejection class only when its text contains one of four phrases:

"trade rejected"  |  "order rejected"  |  "rejection summary"  |  "order not sent"

Your alert says “Order NOT filled”. It matches none of them. I ran your exact message through the shipped functions:

category      : 'other'                                <- not 'rejection'
event_key     : other:19870694:bbf8a6479a6f6259…   <- hashes the FULL message text
rejection key : rejection:19870694                  <- one alert per 90s window

The rejection key deliberately throws away side, lots and reason so that every retry in a window collapses into one message. The other key hashes the entire text — so any variation in the reason string mints a brand-new alert. One word of vocabulary drift disabled the throttle that was built for exactly this case.

The deeper fault

Fixing the word is not enough. _execute_mt5_serialized() calls _news_entry_guard() and returns its block. Back in the runner, any non-ok result is handled as an execution failure:

if result.get("ok"):
    …
else:
    block_msg = str(result.get("message") or "Unknown reason")[:400]
    _telegram_send_text(f"🔴 *GodMode — Order NOT filled*\n…")   <- policy block lands here too

A policy block and an execution failure need opposite handling. A news window is expected, persistent and self-clearing — it should alert once when it starts. A broker rejection is unexpected and should alert every time. R48's intent was right (never leave a DISPATCHED with no outcome); its scope caught policy decisions too. The news window lasts minutes while the scanner retries every couple of seconds, so the alert fires forever.

Part 03 · The biggest lever

Your minimum stop is giving 29% of every trade to the spread

This is the single highest-expectancy change available in the bot, and it is a settings value, not a strategy.

I ran the same entry model over the same 4.08M bars and changed nothing but the stop distance. Gross expectancy — what the market gives you before costs — is flat at every stop size. Net expectancy is not.

The mechanism is arithmetic, not market behaviour. Cost is a fixed number of dollars per round turn. Expressed in R it is inversely proportional to stop distance. A tighter stop does not improve your R:R — it multiplies the toll the spread takes out of every trade. This is also the mechanism behind every negative SMC result in my study: reclaim entries sit close to the swept extreme, so the median stop was $3.05, and $0.35 of spread was 11.5% of risk on every trade.

Your shipped configuration allows a stop far tighter than that.

The rule to add

Replace the fixed point floor with a cost-relative one. A fixed 1.5 cannot track a spread that moves, and your ATR multiples already float correctly — it is only the absolute floor that is broken.

stop_distance >= 20 × (spread + entry_slip + exit_slip)

At 20× the cost is capped at 5% of risk, which is the knee of the curve in Exhibit 3. Below 20 the degradation accelerates sharply; above about 30 you start refusing valid setups for marginal benefit. Prefer widening the stop and resizing the lot over rejecting the setup — that keeps the trade and fixes the economics, but you must resize, or the wider stop silently carries more cash risk than the plan allowed.

Part 04 · The overlay trap

Don't let the session research silence New York

R68.18 implements my findings correctly. But it is about to apply them to a different question than the one they answer.

research_clock_policy sets the New York weight to 0.0. That is right for what it was derived from — holding exposure, where New York produced a −0.03 Sharpe across 11.7 years. Your bot does not hold. It enters and exits inside a session. Those are different measurements and the weight does not transfer between them.

Neither my original study nor the ChatGPT blueprint tested this, so I ran it: a volatility breakout and its exact inverse, in each session, 0.35 ATR stop, 1.5 R target, costs charged, ~2,992 trades per cell.

Three things fall out. First, NY AM is the best intraday cell, not the worst — the exact inverse of what the exposure weight implies. Second, every cell is still negative and the in-sample and out-of-sample signs disagree, so none of this is a strategy; it is evidence about where to point one. Third, and most useful: Asia is the only session where breakout beats fade, while London and New York both prefer fade. That matches the drift result — Asia trends, New York chops — and it is exactly the right shape for an engine selector, which is what the ChatGPT blueprint's orchestrator was designed to be.

Recommendation

Keep researchClockPolicyMode=SHADOW. When you promote it, apply the session weight to risk sizing only, never as an entry on/off switch, and derive a separate intraday weight from your own broker's fills rather than reusing the exposure weight. The two numbers answer different questions and only one of them is about the trades your bot actually makes.

Part 05 · Burst

The ladder escalates into weakness

The burst machinery is genuinely well built. Three gaps remain, and one of them is the shape of the ladder itself.

What is already right: a one-batch-per-campaign lock, ATR-relative stop room (0.55), the armed-retest timing guard from R68.12, per-leg dispatch timestamps that stop a refreshed clock from authorising a chase, and 900-second blocker reminders. That is a more careful execution path than most commercial EAs have.

Invert the ladder

The packaged default is 0.04 → 0.06 → 0.08. That adds more size at worse prices. Both this study and your own blueprint state the rule independently: a burst is a risk-budget allocation, never a recovery ladder. Running it 0.08 → 0.06 → 0.04 puts the largest leg at the best price and makes each add reduce marginal risk. Total basket risk is unchanged; the average entry improves.

Apply the cost gate per leg

Legs currently inherit the base stop distance. Each leg enters later and worse, so an identical stop means later legs carry a higher cost-to-risk ratio than the first. Run Exhibit 4's gate on every leg, not once per batch.

Gate arming on room, not just time

A burst needs continuation to exist. Exhibit 6 is the map: arm where there is room. Gate on the research_clock_policy session plus a live realised-range check against the session's median, so a dead NY afternoon cannot arm a three-leg ladder that has nowhere to go.

What none of this does

None of these changes creates a directional edge, because neither study found one to create. They stop the bot from losing money to avoidable friction and from shouting at you while it does. That is the honest ceiling on what code changes can deliver here — the remaining upside is in execution venue (Exhibit 11 of the first report: viable under $0.35/oz round trip, dead above it) and in patiently accumulating your own broker's fills until you can measure a real edge rather than inherit one.

Your ChatGPT blueprint's acceptance gates — 200–300 clean trades per engine, no year contributing more than 35% of profit, survival under 50–100% slippage stress — are stricter than mine and you should use them as written.
